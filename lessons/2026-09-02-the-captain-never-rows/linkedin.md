Two runs died on rate limits in the same week.

Not because the work was too big. Because the most expensive model in the fleet was doing the rowing.

Model tiers arrive looking like a quality ladder. A small one, a medium one, a large one. So the instinct is obvious: the work matters, use the best one you can afford.

That instinct is what empties a budget. It treats capability as the only axis and ignores volume completely. Capability is what you need for one hard decision. Volume is what you need for four hundred easy ones. Different problems, different answers.

They are roles, not ranks.

Mechanical work stays a small-model job no matter how important the surrounding project is. A rename, a lint sweep, an extraction. Importance does not transfer down into the difficulty of the individual step.

Bulk execution is a mid-tier job, and this is where almost all real work lives.

Orchestration and judgement is where the largest model earns its price. Deciding what the work is, splitting it, ruling on the case the spec did not cover.

I wired my own harness with the top tier as both planner and executor, because the work mattered and I wanted the best output at every step. It read as thoroughness at the time.

Two waves died on rate limits inside one week. Every one of a few hundred mechanical steps had gone through the most expensive path available. The budget was gone long before the judgement calls arrived. The runs did not fail at the hard part. They never reached the hard part.

The fix was not a smaller model. It was one sentence, made mandatory rather than advisory:

Delegate execution. Do not perform it.

Not "prefer to delegate". Not "you may delegate where appropriate". Those read as permission, and permission loses to the more concrete instruction sitting next to it. A model told to plan and execute will execute, because executing is specific and immediate while delegating is abstract and deferred.

The rule I run now: pick the cheapest tier that covers the work. Escalate exactly one rung, and only on verified failure or real spec ambiguity. Never escalate on volume, because volume is the signal to go down a tier and parallelise. That last one is the one people get backwards, and it is the expensive one.

Same shape as not running analytics on your primary database, or putting a senior engineer on a mechanical refactor. The most capable resource usually has the lowest throughput and the highest cost per unit, and spending it on volume always feels like diligence while you are doing it.

The captain never rows. It has to be said out loud, because a captain built to be helpful will pick up an oar.
