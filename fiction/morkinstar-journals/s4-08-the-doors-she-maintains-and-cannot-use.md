# The Doors She Maintains And Cannot Use

> Posted · THE GREAT GAUGE · cleared in 120 ticks

Greetings again, my dear readers.

I have been in the Great Gauge six ticks and I have not got used to the ceilings, which is an embarrassing thing to have to write down. I have stood under a sky with two suns in it and made considerably less of a fuss.

The district is built at the gauge. That is the entire description and everything after it is arithmetic. Doorways nine measures in the clear. Corridors you could run a barge down and still have room to walk beside it. Window reveals so deep that the household on the second landing has put a bed in one and calls it the small room, and it is bigger than most rooms I have slept in. Every public stair here has a rail that starts where the flight starts and then does not stop: not at the landing, not at the turn, not at the top. It carries on around the corner and off down a corridor that has nothing whatever to do with the stair. People rest a hand on it while they walk and let it take them past their own front doors, and nobody has ever mentioned it to me, and I have decided not to be the one who asks.

Nobody here is anywhere near the gauge. The households are small species, mostly, eleven and fourteen and nine to a room, and they are living in volumes that were laid out for four bodies, and they are paying almost nothing for it, and they are delighted. The kitchen in the flat where I did most of my writing has a ladder bolted to it as a permanent fitting, itemised in the tenancy agreement between the sink and the window catch. The letting office counter has a step in front of it for the same reason. The step is also a fitting. It has a number.

Here is the thing I came to write about, and then I did not write about it, and I am going to tell you that now rather than at the end.

Every door in the Great Gauge is certified to full gauge every hundred and twenty ticks.

That is byelaw two hundred and eight and it is not complicated. A door mechanism rated to a gauge has to be demonstrated at that gauge, on a schedule, by a competent person, or the rating lapses, and if the rating lapses the structure it is set in comes off its own certificate, and the certificate runs a Vænheim and nobody wants to be the district that let one go. So on the day, which is today, every door in the district is wound out to its full nine measures and held there for the certified interval and then wound back.

And nothing goes through them. Not one thing. The residents use the small leaf, which is the ordinary person sized door set into the big one, because that is the door they use every day and it is right there, and it would be strange to walk out into the middle of an enormous opening when there is a perfectly good doorway in it. So for one afternoon in every hundred and twenty ticks the whole district stands open at a scale that has nothing to do with anybody in it, and the district goes about its business through the little door, sideways, in twos, the way it always does.

I stood in a residential corridor and watched about forty people not use a doorway. I want to be clear that not one of them found this remarkable, and neither, after the first hour, did I.

[Also posted- The certification day and the resurfacing day are the same day here, because the Great Gauge has one maintenance window and puts everything in it. So this notice goes up on a wall that is due for primer on the same cycle that the doors are due for winding, which means my paper and the entire door stock of the district are on precisely the same schedule, and I have thought about that for slightly longer than it deserves.]

The mechanic came in on the second service of the morning and her name is **Halkri** and she is Hraedh.

I should say plainly that I have written about the Hraedh before, a long time ago, in an entry with rather a lot of graves in it, and I am not going to make anything of that here.

She commutes. Two flicks in, two flicks back, four days out of six, and she has done it for nineteen clicks. She does not live in the Great Gauge. Almost no Hraedh lives in the Great Gauge. I will get to why, and it is not what you are already assuming, and I would like you to hold off assuming it for another page.

The mechanisms are the only things in this district still built to her hand. That is not sentiment, it is a fact about tolerances: the winding gear on a nine measure door has a keyway sized for a grip that can hold the whole leaf steady while a witness mark is read, and the mark has to be read to a hair, and there is no jig for it because a jig would have to be bigger than the door. So it is done by somebody with the hand for it. That somebody comes in on the transit and goes home on the transit.

She let me watch the whole of it, which took most of the morning, and she talked while she worked without ever once looking up, in the way of a person who has explained her job to visitors before and has got the explanation down to the length of the job.

Two things about her hands, because I have to tell you something and her hands are what there is. They are enormous and blunt, all of them except one fine digit on the right, which does everything: it finds the witness mark, it trues the mark, it clears the swarf out of the keyway with a flick. And she scribes her lines with a piece of stone she keeps in a chest pocket. It is a sighting instrument. It is her grandmother's, and her grandmother's before that, and the design on it is older than her people's alphabet, and the reason she has it in her pocket is that it is the flattest thing she owns and a door leaf needs a straight edge.

She said her grandmother worked the far end and could put a stone down to within a third of a degree of a bearing, in the dark, in the cold, on a ridge, by eye and by instrument, all her working life.

I asked what her grandmother would make of the doors.

Halkri said her grandmother would say the doors were easy work indoors, and be pleased for her.

Then she said, and this is the part I want to get down exactly: **I like the job.**

She said it flatly, into the mechanism, while backing off a nut. And then about twenty minutes later, when I had asked one more question with a certain shape to it, she said it again. *I like the job.* And she added, without any edge at all, that she could see what I was doing, and that she would rather I did not.

So I asked her the thing I had been holding all morning, which was how a district built to her gauge ended up with none of her people in it.

She told me to go and ask at the letting office, because they have it printed, and they are better at it than she is.

They do have it printed. The officer at the counter is called **Hulvrin** and it took him about six minutes and he did not hesitate anywhere in it, and when I asked him to go back over the third part he went back over the third part.

It is four lines. The district costs what it costs to run: structure, heat, the air to standard blend, the plant, and the mandated maintenance of every mechanism in it, which at this gauge is the biggest single line on the page. That total is fixed. It does not care who is inside. Then you divide it by the number of residents the volume can lawfully hold, and that is the per resident charge, and that is what appears on a rent notice.

And a room at the gauge lawfully holds one Hraedh household of four, or it lawfully holds thirty of most other people.

So the Hraedh household pays the room. The other household pays a thirtieth of the room. Same room, same certificate, same doors, same byelaw, same everything, and the charge is not aimed at anybody and has no opinion about anybody and would do exactly the same thing to any people in the galaxy who happen to need the whole volume to themselves.

The district was built for them and the arithmetic of building it for them is what priced them out of it. Nobody did that. It is not a policy. There is no committee that voted it and no clause anyone slipped in, and I went and looked, because I always go and look. The scale schedule is published, it is four lines, and every one of the four lines is correct.

[Also posted- I asked Hulvrin whether anyone had ever proposed weighting the charge by volume occupied rather than by head. He said yes, twice, and that both times it was costed, and that both times the cost of recertifying every mechanism in the district under a new charging basis came out larger than the sum being redistributed. He offered to print me the workings. I took them. They are correct too.]

I went back up and told Halkri I could file a correction.

She did not stop working. She asked me, and there was no edge on this either, what I thought that would do.

I told her the truth, my dear readers, because she had earned it and because I have got worse at the other thing. I said: nothing. It will be acknowledged and it will not be actioned, and the acknowledgement will be prompt and courteous and correctly worded, and the number on the rent notice will be the same number.

She said, then do not waste the paper on me.

And then she said: waste it on the stair.

I asked which stair.

She said she reads the wall at the Quarter interchange while she waits for the transit, because there is nothing else to do at the Quarter interchange, and about a momenta ago there was a thing on it about a riser, the fourth one down, and how it was cut wrong before there was a code to cut it wrong by, and how a woman with an old tape had measured a flight that was already wrong. She said she had gone and stood on that stair on the way home and counted down to the fourth one and found it exactly where the paper said it was.

She said: that one is real and somebody can go and put their foot on it. Do that one.

Nobody has ever done that before. Two thousand three hundred entries of people telling me things, and not one of them has ever told me about a thing I wrote.

So I filed the correction on the stair. Not this one. That one. The form is a residency and structures correction and it took the rest of the afternoon at the district office counter, and Hulvrin found me the right one, and there is a field on it for the measured value and a field for the source of the measurement and I put Ondrit's name in it, spelled correctly, and her tape's calibration standard, which is retired, which the form accepted without complaint.

And then I wrote this up and pasted it on the panel by the residential corridor, where the doors were standing open at nine measures for nobody, and where a man on a small ladder had already started priming the far end of the row.

Halkri had gone by then. Two flicks back.

I pressed the bottom corner flat with the heel of my hand and held it a moment while the paste took.
