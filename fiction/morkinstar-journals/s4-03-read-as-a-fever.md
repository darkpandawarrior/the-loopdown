# Read As A Fever

> Posted · ELEVEN HOLD · cleared in 90 ticks

Greetings again, my dear readers. I am on the lower level of platform two in a district called Eleven Hold, I am warm, and I would like to sit inside that fact for one sentence before I get on with anything, because it does not happen to me often.

Warm air falls here. It comes off the exchanger wall at the top of the stack and comes down the stairwells the way water would, which makes the lower level the good level and the upper level the cheap one, so a room down here lets for about a fifth more than the identical room two flights up. That is how the district is priced. I asked three people about it on my first afternoon and all three of them answered the question I had asked, which was about rent, and not the one I meant, and they were right to, because the one I meant is not a question anybody in Eleven Hold has.

Where it shows up is in the fittings, and you need that before you get the rest of it. The citywide schedule puts the extract grille in the ceiling and the supply low, which is correct in every district where the air goes the other way. Here it means the warmth is drawn off at exactly the height it arrives at and the cool is fed in at the ankle. This was noticed at the merge and a compensating measure was granted: a run of radiant heaters along the underside of the platform canopy, over the edge, pointed down at the line where people actually stand. Those heaters are the only warmth on this platform that ever reaches a person.

Last cycle they went to a seasonal schedule.

[Also posted- the ceiling above platform two is, by a wide margin, the most comfortable surface in this district. I have no use for that information and neither do you and I am giving it to you anyway.]

The people here are Ombri, Ombra's, and some of you read me on them a very long time ago and I am not going to file that entry twice. The short of it is that they run hot, high enough that a medic who has never met one will start hunting for the infection, and that when they meet you they take hold of you with both arms and count eleven. Avn, dur, treskh, and up from there. They settled this stack because it backs onto an exchanger and because the rent suited them, in that order, and there are enough of them now that every bowl stall at the head of the stairs is theirs and the queue smells like the inside of somebody's coat.

The woman who checked my stub at the barrier reached for me, and there was nothing there to take hold of, and she said sorry the way you say sorry to a door that has already opened for you, and asked me where I was trying to get to. I said I was not trying to get anywhere. She said that was allowed.

Now, the thing.

Since the canopy heaters went to a seasonal schedule, the public health monitor on platform two has logged three Ombri a winter as medical incidents and dispatched a response to each of them. Not three in total. Three a winter, two winters running, six people, all of whom were sitting on a bench waiting for a service, and all of whom were entirely well.

The monitor is not broken, and I want that said flatly before I go on, because I know exactly what this sounds like and it is not that. It takes a surface reading off the waiting line, compares it against the recorded normal for the species it is looking at, and escalates when the reading sits far enough under that normal for long enough. It has done that six times, correctly, and a response has come out six times, correctly, and there is a sign on the pillar which reads THIS PLATFORM IS MONITORED FOR PUBLIC HEALTH. RESPONSE IS AUTOMATIC AND IS NOT A CHARGE TO YOU, and that is true as well.

The recorded normal is the problem. The recorded normal is also right, in the only sense the word is doing any work here, which is that it is an honest average of the readings that existed.

Sulvik schedules health monitoring for four districts on this tier and he took me through it at a counter, standing, in about six minutes, without once saying he would have to check. The spec sets a baseline per species. The baselines were not surveyed at the merge, because surveying ten thousand species costs what it costs and there was already a dataset sitting there that had numbers about bodies in it. So the baselines came out of the corpus that was bought. The Ombri line in the spec cites its source, the way a spec has to, and Sulvik read the citation off the screen to me in a completely level voice, because there is no reason on any world why it should have meant anything to him.

Entry two two six nine.

I know what is in that entry. I know because I wrote it, on Killuga Var, and I was pleased with it at the time, and I have not thought about the ledger in it since the afternoon I copied it out.

Here is what a cold hold is, briefly, and then I will stop. When an Ombri takes hold of you and counts, they are not greeting you. They are checking whether you are warm at eleven, because the thing they have been checking for since the Wearing does not make heat of its own. If you are cold at eleven, the procedure is not to let go. The procedure is to keep holding and keep counting, on the grounds that half of Ombra is heat and you are carrying some of it and the thing in your arms is not, and you go on for as long as you can stand. It has never once worked. They are still doing it, and they still teach it to their children as the first thing after walking.

And a cold hold is the only occasion in the whole history of that world on which anybody ever wrote a body temperature down, because it is the only occasion on which the number is worth anything. That is what the ledger is. Ædri put the first line of it on a hearth-stone and every line since has been added under hers.

So the figure this city holds for what an Ombri body ought to be doing is an average of readings taken off people at the top of their output, arms locked around something with nothing in it, in the middle of the worst thing that happens to them, refusing to let go.

Set against that figure, an Ombri sitting on a bench in a district whose canopy heaters are off for the season reads as failing.

I said I would like to file a correction. Sulvik said yes of course, and then told me, kindly and with none of the tone that sentence usually arrives with, that one was already on the list. Filed three cycles ago by a technician on the monitoring contract who had come at it from the other end and got there first. Costed. Approved in principle. Referred to the next budget cycle, which is where a correct thing goes when it is not urgent, and it is not urgent because the response works and nobody has been harmed. He offered me the reference number in case I wanted to add to it. I took the number down. I did not have anything to add. It was a better correction than mine would have been.

[Also posted- the reference number has an accession stamp on it and so does the enamel plate on the handrail at the head of the stairs, which reads ACC 01 and nothing else, and I have asked two people what it is for and been told, both times, that it is for the rail.]

The ledger is still running. That is the part I came down here to write about and I have taken four hundred words to get to it, which is about standard for me.

It runs out of a room off the lower concourse the size of a service cupboard, kept by a woman named Ædrin, who is Ombri, and who has had it for a hundred and forty momenta. It is a municipal continuation now. It is an open dataset with a publication endpoint, a retention term of one Vænheim, renewable, and a maintenance window between the third and fourth hour on every eighth tick, which lines up with nothing in this city's day, because it was set by a body whose day is not this city's day.

What she logs is holds. The occasions have changed. When the canopy heaters are off, the rail along the platform edge goes cold, properly cold, and an Ombri going past puts a hand on it, and a hand on a cold thing gets both hands and a count, because that is what you do with a cold thing you are touching and you learned it before you learned anything else. Four hundred and six entries since the schedule changed. Every one of them a rail.

Before she writes an entry, Ædrin goes out to the platform and takes hold of the rail herself, both hands, and counts. This is not in the procedure. The log has a field for the count reached and she will not put a figure in that field she did not get with her own hands, and getting it takes nine seconds, and she does it in the middle of a shift with people walking round her and nobody looks twice, because there is nothing there to look at.

The rail has never been warm at eleven. She told me that the way you would tell somebody a service was running late.

She writes the sheet up by hand first, flat against her forearm with the flange folded back under it to make a desk, and types it into the endpoint at the end of the shift, because she does not like a hold sitting unwritten while a server is down. She put the current sheet into my hand so I could read the last four entries and let go of it before I had it, which is a thing you learn to do around me fairly quickly.

I asked her who reads the dataset. She said she had assumed for a long time that somebody must, and then one quarter she went and looked, because it is published and the figure is published with it, and the number of parties drawing on that endpoint is nought. She said it as a fact about a server. It has not changed anything about how she keeps it. She gave me the end of the bench nearest the stair, which is where the falling air comes down and is the warm end, and she took the other one.

Nobody has ever asked her what the ledger is for.

I went through the concourse that afternoon and under the board, the way you do, and it read eighteen where it read nineteen the last time I was under it. So I came back down here and pulled the closure register at the terminal by the lower barrier, which is published like everything else in this city, and the file that had come off was a drainage survey four districts over, closed because a household there came into coverage and the last field got filled. Somebody did their job and a good thing happened to people I will never meet.

The Ombri baseline is still open. I checked that too. Status against the reference Sulvik gave me: approved in principle, referred.

I wrote this on the bench with the falling air on the back of my neck, and then I walked it down to the panel by the lower barrier, which is resurfaced every ninety ticks and was last done a good while ago by the look of it. Ædrin came out as far as the top of the stair to see where I was putting it, and went back in.

Then I put my thumb on the bottom corner, where it always lifts, and held it there until it stayed.
