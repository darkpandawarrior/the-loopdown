# It Rains At Fourteen Past

> Posted · THE COVERED LEVELS · cleared in 66 ticks

Greetings again, my dear readers. Lu'kifær Morkinstar, fourth of the Covered Levels, writing this on a bench at the transit dock with wet sleeves and a folded annex in my hand that I have decided not to be brave about.

The Covered Levels are what the name says and nothing cleverer. Six decks stacked under the plant tier, ceilinged in grating, floored in grating, lit along the rails rather than overhead, and warm in the specific way a place is warm when its warmth is somebody else's exhaust. You come down into them and the sound changes, because everything above you is a void and everything below you is another one. Then the Common bands.

Coverage on this level is partial and the tier stamp says so at the head of every stair. What partial does, in practice, is that a person eight paces off resolves cleanly and the same person at twenty paces goes soft at the edges and comes back looking a little more like the last person you resolved, which is what an instrument does when it is short of what it needs. Nobody down here experiences that as a loss. It is the level, the way a draught is the level. They walk closer.

I have no rendering at all, of course, which is a form number and not a mystery, and which on a half served level makes me about the sixth most inconvenient thing about the afternoon. They walked closer. They said the name first.

Now the wrong thing, and I want to state it flatly before anybody's grandmother gets involved.

It rains indoors here at fourteen minutes past, every tick, without exception.

I measured it, because measuring is the only thing I am actually qualified to do. Seven consecutive ticks, three positions, one directly under the east grate bank. It begins at fourteen past. It runs four minutes and a little, tapering rather than stopping. It comes down out of the ceiling fine and cold, colder than the air by a noticeable margin, lands on floor grating and goes away, and eleven minutes later there is no evidence it happened except the smell, which is good, and which nobody who lives here can smell any more.

It is not a leak and it is not doing anything the level was not built to have it do. Stalls have awnings. Nobody moves. That is the part I want you to have properly, because I got it wrong for two ticks and stood about waiting for a reaction: at fourteen past on this level a rain comes down through the ceiling and roughly nobody looks up.

[Also posted- I asked eleven people on this level what time it rains and got fourteen past from all eleven, including one who was asleep and answered anyway. I asked the same eleven when the plant above them is serviced and got four different answers, three shrugs, and one extremely confident wrong one. I think this is the correct allocation of attention and I would do exactly the same]

The teller is **Ashvi**, who has a stall at the east head and who has put a bowl out at fourteen past since she was small.

Not to catch anything. This matters and she made sure I had it. The bowl is shallow, glazed, chipped at the rim in one place and never replaced, and when she sets it down she puts two fingers underneath and one on the rim so it does not ring on the grating. Afterwards she takes it in and dries it, wet or not, on a cloth kept for that. I watched her do this eleven times. On the fourth day she moved the cloth so I could sit down, and said nothing about it.

She gave me the story on the second tick, in the gaps between people wanting things from her, which is the only way I have ever got a story worth having.

Nine generations ago a woman on this level said out loud that it was going to rain here at fourteen past. There was no rain then. There was a level, and grating, and the plant above it, and a woman who said a thing that was not true in front of enough people that it could not be taken back. And then it rained at fourteen past, and has ever since, and that is the whole of it.

Ashvi has all nine generations in the right order and gave them to me in order, exact in the way people are about a thing they were made to learn young. The first said it. The second started putting a bowl out, and Ashvi is clear that the second is the important one and that everybody who tells this badly gets that wrong. The third sold bowls at the tier head, which she reports without embarrassment as the generation that made money. The fourth stopped. The fifth started again. Six, seven and eight came in one breath because, in her words, nothing happened to them, they just kept the bowl. The ninth is her.

They do not keep the woman's name.

I asked four ways over three ticks. The answer that landed came from a man at the west head who repairs awnings: they keep the saying and not the sayer, because if you kept the name you would have to keep whose she was, and then you would be keeping a family and not a rain.

So I asked Ashvi, finally, straight out, whether she thinks it is true. That a woman said it and it came.

She was drying the bowl. She said the story is not for being true.

I asked what it is for. She thought about it properly, which I have learned to be grateful for, and said that when she was small the bowl went out because her mother put it out, and now it goes out because she puts it out, and that if I wanted better than that she did not know anybody who had it. Then she asked me if I was all right.

I said I was fine, which was true at the time.

Here is the mechanism. I got it from **Tœvik** at the facilities counter on the plant tier, in about six minutes without hesitating, on a question she has been asked four times in the life of the contract, three by insurers.

The exchanger bank above takes heat off the levels below it and runs a purge on a fixed cycle. A purge dumps its condensate into the overhead void, because a void with drainage in it is the cheapest place in a building to put water you do not want. The void drains through the ceiling grating. That is the rain, all of it, and it is the design, not an accident, and the runs under the floor were sized for it before anybody lived here.

The cycle is not keyed to the tick. It is keyed to the plant standard the bank was certified under, whose base interval is shorter than a tick and will not divide into one, and the offset between the two was worked out once, restated at the merge, and not touched since. Fourteen past is what falls out of that arithmetic, and it will keep falling out of it until the bank is recertified, which is a schedule and not an intention.

Tœvik keeps a printed copy of the annex in the counter drawer, because the terminal renders it without the offset column, which she has reported and which has been acknowledged. She squared the sheets against the counter edge, turned them round, pushed them across with the back of one hand rather than the front of it, and said I could keep that copy, she prints two.

And yes, I checked whether the fourteen was a fourteen. It is not. It is what is left over when one interval is converted into another that will not take it evenly, and I have been at this long enough to know a remainder when somebody prints one for me. I noted it and left it alone.

The contract is public. That is the part I keep going back to. Facilities Services Agreement, Covered Levels, Schedule 6, Annex C, revision eleven. It is in the register, requestable by anybody standing at that counter, and it took me an hour, most of which was walking.

Nine generations. An hour.

I had that annex folded in my hand while Ashvi was telling me about the eighth generation, and I did not take it out, and I did not say anything, and I would like to give you the honest reason rather than a presentable one. I do not have either. I have written the sentence four ways on this bench and none of the four is true. It is not that I was protecting anything. There is nothing here to protect: the rain is real, it will keep coming, and knowing why takes nothing off the level. Nobody has ever been forbidden anything. They simply have not asked, and I did, and I still could not open my mouth, and I am telling you that plainly rather than dressing it up as a decision.

[Also posted- the stall at the west head sells bowls. The plain ones are bowls. The ones with the glaze that clouds when it is wet are sold as fourteen past bowls at considerably more than a bowl. I have decided not to have an opinion about that, having spent this entire entry being the sort of person who looks under things]

The signage at the east head, since the city says it better than I do. COVERED LEVEL FOUR. THIS LEVEL DISCHARGES AT PLUS FOURTEEN. STAND CLEAR OF FLOOR GRATES. AIR TO STANDARD BLEND. COVERAGE ON THIS LEVEL IS PARTIAL. REPRESENTATIVE, NOT EXACT. THIS SURFACE IS RESURFACED EVERY 66 TICKS. And on the plate at the foot of the plant riser, at a height where you would have to crouch, a class letter and ACCESSION 06, which I did not ask about, having learned that here, asking about a number on a fitting gets it read back to you slowly.

There is one of the boards at the dock. I looked up on the way past, which is what everybody does, and which is presumably why they put the thing at that height.

Files Open, seventeen. It was eighteen when I came down here. The lines are listed, because seventeen is a number you can list, and the one that is gone is the fare gate at the far end of the Long Concourse, the one that cycled open for nobody once a shift, which I wrote up and posted on a wall three districts back. Somebody fixed it. Somebody else certified that somebody had fixed it. The last field went in, the file closed, and I was down here in the wet getting a bowl story.

I am not going to pretend that did not feel good, my dear readers, because it did, and it is the first thing in a long while that has.

Ashvi came down to the dock, cloth still over her shoulder, having closed the stall to do it, which I did not work out until afterwards, and asked me a second time whether I was all right.

I said the same thing as the first time. She looked at me for a bit longer than the answer needed, then said the bowl would be out at fourteen past tomorrow, and went back up.

So I wrote this on the bench, then went back up and pasted it at the east head, at a height a person walking past can read without stopping.

The bottom corner lifted while I was still standing there. I pressed it back down with the flat of my thumb and held it until it stayed.
