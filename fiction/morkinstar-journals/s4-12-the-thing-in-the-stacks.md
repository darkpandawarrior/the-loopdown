# The Thing In The Stacks

> Posted · THE HOLDING TIERS · cleared in 8 ticks

Greetings again, my dear readers.

The lift down to the Holding Tiers runs four minutes without stopping and the only thing to look at on the way is a plate on the gear housing reading ACC 22, and nothing else. I read it perhaps thirty times. I have got out of the habit of asking about numbers on fittings in this city, because asking gets you the number read back to you slowly and kindly by somebody who has better things to do.

What is at the bottom of the lift is a floor. I want to be honest about the poverty of that description, because it is the accurate one. There is a floor. The floor has racking on it, nine tiers of it, and the racking goes off in both directions to well past the point where a lamp is any use. Between the racks there is an aisle wide enough for a trolley and a person walking beside it, and down the middle of the aisle at hand height there is a rail that does not stop. Not at the cross aisle, not at the lift. It goes around the lift and carries on.

I cannot tell you how big this place is, because I have not seen it and there is no arrangement of my body, or of anything I have instead of one, that would let me. What I can tell you is what is published, since everything here is published: the Holding Tiers appears as an extent in the same schedule that carries the resurfacing cycles, and the figure given for that extent is about the size of a small moon. I have written that phrase before, a very long time ago, about somewhere else entirely. I noticed. I am moving on.

Here is the thing I came down to look at, and it is not a mystery, it is a notice.

The floors are being reinforced. Not repaired. Reinforced, aisle by aisle and tier by tier, on a rolling programme with no end date, because it is not the kind of programme that has one. And the reason for it is posted at the head of every aisle, refreshed every quarter, in the city's own lettering at the city's own height, because it is a safety notice and the law is very clear about where a safety notice goes. It gives the current load on this floor, measured, and beside it the rated load, certified, and beside that the date of the next survey.

The current figure is higher this quarter than last quarter. It was higher last quarter than the quarter before. It has been higher every quarter since somebody first thought to write it down, and it will be higher next quarter, and every single person down here knows this the way you know which way water runs.

Nothing in this city is ever thrown away. I want to put that flatly and with no freight on it, because it is not a scandal, it is a procedure, and it is a good one. A record here is not destroyed, it is superseded. A superseded record is filed. Filing is a place on a shelf. A place on a shelf is a load on a floor. So the record has a weight, and the weight goes one way, and the floors are taken under it on a schedule, and the schedule is funded, and everybody involved is doing their job correctly.

The signage at the head of nine tier, since the city says it better than I do. THE HOLDING TIERS. STORAGE TIER 9. THIS FLOOR IS RATED. DO NOT EXCEED THE POSTED LOAD. THE POSTED LOAD IS ON THE PILLAR. COVERAGE IS NOT PROVIDED AT THIS ADDRESS. THIS SURFACE IS RESURFACED EVERY 8 TICKS. Then the civic parcel number, then the byelaw, then nothing.

That fifth line is worth one sentence and no more than one. The Common is billed to addresses and a storage tier is not an address, so nobody working down here is served, and the hands do the job with a lamp, a rail, a manifest board and a raised voice, which is what the job has always been done with. Nobody mentioned it to me. It is on the plate.

[Also posted- the aisle numbering on nine tier runs to a scheme that was superseded before anybody now working here started, and the superseded scheme is itself filed somewhere on nine tier, and I asked whether anyone had ever gone and got it out and read it, and was told that they had, once, and that it had been very interesting, and that they had then put it back, because putting it back is the only thing you can do with it.]

The stacks hand who took me round is called **Osvær** and he has worked nine tier for nineteen clicks.

He carries his manifest boards under one arm, six or seven of them at a time, and he counts a shelf by walking the pad of his thumb along the spines of the boxes without looking at them at all, and that thumb is worn pale and flat and faintly shiny, the way the knuckle of a woman I met on my first day in this city is worn from carrying a bucket hooked over it. He is fast. He is fast in the way of somebody who long ago stopped experiencing his own speed as a skill, and when I asked him to slow down so I could see what he was doing he slowed down and then, within about a minute, sped up again, and apologised, and slowed down again, and we did that four times and both found it funny.

He told me about the thing in the stacks on the second morning, in the tone you use for a joke you have told a great many times and still like.

There is a thing in the deep aisles. It has no body of its own. Its body is what is filed. Every line you write on a manifest makes it bigger by exactly that much, and there is no way to write a line that does not, and it does not read them and does not care in the least what any of them say. It is not going through your paperwork looking for errors. It is only getting heavier. So do not write a line you did not have to write, and do not file a thing twice, and check the box is what the board says it is before you put the board on it.

That is what they tell new starters. Osvær had it told to him nineteen clicks ago by a woman who has since retired, and he tells it now, and he told me he tells it because a new starter who is frightened of the deep aisles is a new starter who writes a careful manifest, and a careful manifest is the whole job.

I am not going to put its name in this notice, my dear readers. Some of you have been with me since Cendre and you know the name and you know the rule that comes attached to it, and the rule is the entire reason I am not writing it down. That is all I am going to say about that.

Then he said, and this is the part he had been waiting to get to: but we do actually measure it, and I will show you.

It is an eleven minute walk. He did not tell me it was an eleven minute walk. He walked it, talking the whole way about a boxing standard that changed nine clicks ago and made every box on four tier one third of a measure taller, which he thinks was correct and still resents, and then he stopped at a pillar that looks exactly like the four hundred pillars before it and put his lamp on it.

There are cuts in the concrete. A short horizontal cut, clean, about the length of a finger, with a small number stamped beside it, and above it another, and above that another, going up the face of the pillar in a column to somewhere above my reach.

He said: this is where we measure it. One a year. The deep hands cut it, the ones who work the far end, and they cut it at the turn of the year, and this year's is that one.

And he put his thumb in this year's cut. Nobody asked him to. He did it while he was still talking, the way you put your hand on a gatepost you have known a long time, and left it there.

There is a plate at the foot of that pillar at about knee height. It reads STRUCTURAL MONITORING POINT 9/4471. DATUM SET AT COMMISSIONING. DEFLECTION SURVEYED QUARTERLY UNDER THE REINFORCEMENT PROGRAMME. Then a contractor's name and a licence class and a contact for the programme office. I read it twice, and I read the column of cuts again, and then Osvær took his thumb off the pillar and we walked back, and he told me the rest of the boxing standard, and I let him.

The programme office is on the tier above and the officer who runs the paperwork for it is called **Kaldri**, and she gave me six minutes and did not hesitate anywhere in them, and she was right, and when I asked her to go back over the second part she went back over the second part.

The reinforcement programme is costed over its whole life. Because it has no end date, whole life means in perpetuity, which is a real thing you can put a figure on if you are careful about your assumptions, and she is careful about hers, and they are printed at the top of the page. That is column one.

And every year, because a responsible authority tests its own programme against the alternative, somebody prepares column two. Column two is what it would cost to do the other thing: a general unweighting across the jurisdiction, everything reviewed, everything not required released, the consultation that a release of that size legally requires, the migration, the write off of what cannot be migrated, and the staff to do all of it. It has been prepared eleven times. Column two has been larger than column one every one of those eleven times, and the difference has widened every one of those eleven times, and Kaldri has prepared four of them herself and does the totals longhand once before she trusts the machine, which takes her two days, and she has never yet found a discrepancy, and she does it again the next year.

The deferral is signed at the foot of the page by somebody competent, and this year it is signed by her, and she told me without any prompting that she would sign it again, and that it is the correct decision.

She gave me the page. It is a public document. Anybody may have one.

Column one: 1,908,400.

Column two: 4,377,100.

That is the entire entry. I am not going to write underneath it what I think it means.

On the way out Osvær took a fresh board off the stack by the lift and wrote a line on it, in a small square hand, and told me it was the rule: everybody who comes onto a tier goes on the tier's manifest for the day, name, aisle, time in, time out. He asked me what to put. I told him, and he wrote it, and spelled it correctly on the first attempt, which almost nobody does.

Then he filed the board.

I pasted this at the head of nine tier, on a works board that is primed every eight ticks, which is the shortest cycle I have come across anywhere in this city.

The bottom corner was lifting. I put my thumb on it and pressed it flat.
