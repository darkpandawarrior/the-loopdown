# The Last Thing He Taught Them

> Page 16 of 91

Greetings again, my dear readers. Sixteen slots filled now, out of ninety one, and I want to be honest with you about something before I get into today's world, which is that I picked up a piece of wood on the ninth slot and could not for the life of me remember what I meant to put in it. That has never happened before. I am telling you because it felt important and I do not yet know why.

Today's world is Ilmarrow, and I need you to sit down for this one, because I very nearly did not write it at all, on the grounds that I did not think I could do it without ruining it, and I have decided that the risk of ruining it is better than the certainty of not telling you.

The Directory files the people of Ilmarrow as Ilmarrans, which is the sort of tidy administrative noun a surveyor writes down when he is in a hurry and the locals have not yet been asked. They call themselves the **Rethi**. It translates, as close as the rig will get it, to "the ones he taught," and once you know why, you will never be able to hear the word any other way again, which is the whole point of it.

Ilmarrow has a wobble problem. Every world spins on a tilt and every tilt drifts a little, that is just physics being physics, but most worlds drift slowly enough that nobody notices in a lifetime, or a hundred lifetimes. Ilmarrow's tilt is aggressive. Left alone it walks by whole degrees inside a single generation, and when a planet's tilt walks, its growing season walks with it. The month that used to be planting month becomes, in fifty clicks, the month you would plant in if you wanted every seed to freeze. This is not weather being unkind. This is the actual geometry of the world sliding out from under the calendar, and a species that farms on a schedule cannot survive a schedule that will not hold still.

For as far back as the Rethi keep records, the tilt did not walk, because something held it. The Rethi call that something Hallovar. I am going to describe him the way three separate Rethi described him to me, which is not as a figure of light or a booming voice in the sky, but as a very specific, very physical presence: a weight, felt through the ground, that corrected. Farmers on Ilmarrow will tell you, flatly, the way you'd tell someone which way is north, that on the mornings the tilt tried to slip you could feel it slip and then feel it get put back, like a table leg someone had just kicked square again. That is Hallovar's whole job description. He holds the seasons. Not symbolically. He is the correction.

Three hundred clicks before the end, Hallovar told them the exact hour he was going to stop.

I want to be precise about what that was and was not, because every version of this story that leaves Ilmarrow gets it wrong in the same direction, and it is a direction that makes it a sadder, smaller story than the one that actually happened. Hallovar did not ask anyone to kill him. Nobody killed him. Nobody was ever asked to do anything to him at all. He simply announced, to a room of specific named people whose names the Rethi still keep, that in three hundred clicks, to the hour, he would stop, and that this was not a request, a threat, or a test. It was a fact about him, the way a candle running out is a fact about the candle, and he had apparently always known the hour and had simply never mentioned it before because there had never before been anything useful to do with the information.

Now there was something useful to do with it. He had three hundred clicks and a room full of people, and he spent every one of the clicks teaching them how to do his job.

This is the part I want you to sit with, because it is not a story about a sacrifice. Nobody sacrificed anything. It is a story about an extremely long, extremely specific, extremely well organised course of instruction, delivered by someone who was very good at his subject and only middling at patience, to students who were not, by his own repeated written admission, keeping up.

He wrote the whole thing out as a syllabus. I have held it. I will get to that. Three hundred and forty numbered lessons, each with a stated aim, a method, and a piece of homework, because he made them do homework, my dear readers, actual homework, on a fixed schedule, while the god who assigned it stood over the desk and told them to do it again.

Lesson twelve is called *Reading The Lean Before It Moves.* Lesson eighty eight is called *What The Rain Does To Your Counterweights And Why You Checked Them Wrong.* There is a lesson, number one hundred and three, whose homework was simply the word "again," underlined twice, in handwriting a scribe on Ilmarrow can still show you, because he had made the same three students repeat the same shadow measurement four evenings running and on the fifth evening he had apparently had enough of writing full sentences about it.

He was not a kind teacher. I want to be fair to him and to the Rethi both, because the Rethi are fair to him, more than most children are fair to a hard teacher, and they are fair to him because they understand, in a way I did not until I stood in that room, that his impatience cost him something to show. A god with three hundred clicks and forty generations of pattern to hand to sixty mortal students who could each only hold about a decade of it before their hands got too old to be steady does not have room for gentleness he cannot afford. He sighed. He made them redo things. He wrote, in one surviving margin, "you are measuring the wrong side of the stone, we have discussed this," in a hand you can tell is tired even if you cannot read the words. And they loved him. Every account I collected on Ilmarrow includes at least one complaint about him, offered with a kind of pride I have only otherwise heard from people talking about a parent, which is its own kind of answer to the question of whether he was kind.

What they built, working from his three hundred and forty lessons, is a structure the Rethi call the Standing Wheel, a gimballed mass of dressed stone and cast bronze set into the shoulder of Ilmarrow's tallest range, spun and re-weighted by crews who work it in shifts the way you'd work a mill, and it does, mechanically, by momentum and counterweight and a great deal of very tired arithmetic, approximately what Hallovar did by simply being there. Approximately. Not exactly. The Wheel corrects the tilt to within a measurable tolerance, and every generation the tolerance is not quite enough, and the season creeps.

They have a word for the creep. It is not a curse word and nobody says it bitterly. They call it the **Stutter**, because the drift shows up first, every time, in the same place it always showed up first in Hallovar's own handwriting near the end of the three hundred clicks: small, at the edges, a season arriving four days late where it used to arrive on the day, then six, then nine, a hand not quite steady on something that used to be sure. The Wheel is eighty percent of him, they told me, more than once, in those words, and they have made their peace with the other twenty percent the way you make peace with anything you were handed slightly unfinished by someone who ran out of time and not out of care.

He ended on schedule. Not near it. On it, to the stated hour, three hundred clicks after he named it, in the middle of a lesson, having taught everything he had planned to teach in the order he had planned to teach it, without hurrying the last weeks to cram anything extra in and without slowing the last weeks to stretch anything out. He set down the tool he had been using to true a counterweight, did not pick up another, said nothing further to the room, and was, in the plain words of the man who told me this and was three generations removed from anyone who had been there, not there any more. That is the whole account. No speech. No last words that anyone kept. Just a scheduled stop, arrived at on schedule, by someone who had apparently decided that lingering past his own deadline would be a kind of dishonesty he did not want to leave behind him.

He had written three hundred and forty lessons.

The syllabus, in his own hand, lists three hundred and forty one.

Lesson three hundred and forty one has a title and nothing under it. The title is *What To Do When It Goes Wrong And I Am Not Here.*

Every Rethi child learns that title the way children on other worlds learn a prayer, by heart, before they are old enough to understand what it is promising. It is, without any real competition, the most sacred object on Ilmarrow, and it is a heading with no body. He ran out of clicks. Three hundred and forty lessons fit inside three hundred clicks and the three hundred and forty first did not, and there is no version of the story, in any household on that planet, where he cut an earlier lesson short to make room for it. He taught the ones he had time to teach completely, in full, to the standard he clearly held for himself, and arrived at the last hour of the last click one lesson short, the way you arrive at the bottom of a page with one more sentence left to write and no more page.

They have been trying to write it for him for four thousand clicks. There is a room on Ilmarrow, plain stone, no different from a granary, where every attempt anyone has ever made at Lesson three hundred and forty one is kept. Farmers' attempts. A child's attempt, six lines long, that a scribe there let me copy and then asked me, very seriously, not to circulate, and I am keeping that promise, my dear readers, so do not write in. Whole committees have drafted versions across whole generations. Every single one is kept. Not one has ever been authorised, because authorising one would mean deciding that somebody other than Hallovar gets to finish Hallovar's syllabus, and in four thousand clicks, nobody on Ilmarrow has been willing to be the one who decides that.

I have seen a blank line kept sacred before. On Marlt they keep a fourteenth line on a list with nothing written on it, because the alternative was worse than the blank. On the Væhn homeworld they burn everything they own every ninth generation so a six year old can hand them one page that has never existed before, because the alternative to something new is nothing at all. I did not expect to find a third one, and I did not expect it to be a syllabus, and I did not expect, when the woman who keeps that plain stone room, whose name is Ilvra and whose family has kept it for six generations, asked me if I wanted to hold the original, that my hands would actually shake.

I held it. It is heavier than three hundred and forty lessons of paper should be, though I suspect that is not a fact you could put a scale to.

I did not have a speech. I am not sure there is a version of Lu'kifær Morkinstar with a speech that would have belonged in that room. I looked at the title of Lesson three hundred and forty one for a long time, in his own hand, from before it started to shake, and I gave the syllabus back to Ilvra with both hands the way she had handed it to me, and the only thing I said, which I mean more plainly than I have meant anything I have written in fifteen years of this job, was that I hoped somebody finished it. Not that somebody would. Just that I hoped it.

I am going to leave it there.
